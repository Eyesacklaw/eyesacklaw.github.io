Weekly Summary

- Starmer resigns as prime minister.

- Manufacturing jobs in the US fell (tracked by the S&P Global's US Flash Purchasing Managers' Index), despite demand for self-reliance on certain industries.

- On Sunday, Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz in response to Israel and Hizbollah exchanging fire, which it views as a violation of the Memorandum of Understading (MoU).

- Growing discontent from the Senate causes them to pass a war powers resolution, attempting to stabilise Trump's unhinged policies. In essence, the resolution instructs Trump to end the war or request permission from Congress to continue.

- Hawkish FED has caused traders to bet on interest rate rises in the future, strengthening the dollar against other currencies, lowering bond and commodity prices.

- Central banks across the world remain committed to combat inflation, even after the ceasefire and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

- Opec expects demand for oil to continue rising in the next 25 years, due to weaker international climate action, and resilent demand from cars, trucks and ships (Mundy).

Updates on Individual Stocks

- SpaceX has been awarded the lowest ESG score by MSCI. Triple C due to concerns about governance, including limited shareholder rights, Musk retaining supervoting shares, and conflict of interests (Hodgson). This could lead to funds which need to maintain a certain ESG requirement to exclude SpaceX from their portfolios. As of the time of writing, SpaceX has fallen 30% from its all time high since IPO.

Uncertainty and distrust

At the top of the page of Observations, there is a section on market updates which provides a neat summary of our global economy. Lately, you could compress it into two words: uncertainty and distrust.

Start with uncertainty. Despite the ceasefire agreeement being signed by US and Iran, suggesting de-escalation,the underlying situation is less clean. The Strait of Hormuz remains closed (to date), proxy conflicts between Lebanon and Israel continue, and yet oil prices are falling on the assumption that everything will resolve itself. That leaves inflation in a slightly awkward place; it's caught between geopolitical risk and market optimism. Central banks, meanwhile, are hawkish, or at least signaling that they might be, which raises the question of whether policy is actually useful or will the market just cool on its own.

We also have distrust in the economy, from the departure of Starmer to the Senate trying to legally control Trump and prevent volatility in markets. Internationally, there's also distrust. Last week I mentioned that countries are repatriating gold reserves back to their own country. They are also massively investing into infrastructure for self-reliance of critical industries, exemplified by EU countries joining Pax Silica to prevent reliance on Chinese AI supply chains (Sevastopulo). Of course, countries have been spending more on defence. Markets can tolerate uncertainty; they are less comfortable with distrust.

For investors, the implication is not necessarily an immediate drawdown, but a shift in the distribution of outcomes. More volatility, fewer clean narratives, and probably lower future returns, particularly in the parts of the market that have benefited most from stability, globalization, and cheap capital.

In this macroeconomic environment, there are ways to respond, but none are especially exciting. You can rotate into sectors with inelastic demand and predictable cash flows, such as consumer staples (VDC is a cheap consumer staples ETF by vanguard), where revenues are less sensitive to macroeconomic conditions. Obviously, it's a bit boring, but you'll have to trade lower volatility for modest, single-digit returns.
