The hype of data centres

With basically every tech company heavily investing in AI and data centres, investors often question whether such "investments" will ultimately pay off. For now, it appears somewhat rational, albeit a risky bet, largely hinging on how persistent demand for AI and cloud services proves to be. From an economics perspective, firms that control large-scale compute infrastructure benefit from strong economies of scale and increased productivity. However, the speed and magnitude of investment raise concerns about potential overinvestment in the short-term, especially given how capital-intensive and slow-moving these projects are. Some investors have compared these investments in AI to the telecom bubble in the early 2000s, where, although long-term value was eventually realized, it only occured after periods of excess and inefficiency. Although the overall direction of data centre investments seems rational, predicting which specific companies will lead in this race is extremely difficult. Maybe buying the entire tech index ETF is a better way to bet on this trend.

About the merger...

Although ParknShop and Wellcome are perhaps not well known on a global scale, they make up a huge proportion of Hong Kong's supermarket industry. To the US audience, think of it as Costco and Walmart merging together, or for those in the UK, think of them as Tesco and Sainsbury. Naturally, the potential merger has triggered concerns about a monopoly, given that the two chains were estimated to account for roughly 90% of Hong Kong’s supermarket sector as of 2023 (Lam). At face value, that kind of concentration suggests the risk of monopoly pricing and worsening affordability for Hong Kong consumers. However, the companies themselves argue their combined market share is below 50%, which points to a broader shift in how this “market” is defined. How can we explain this difference? and where has all the spending gone to?

This discrepancy can perhaps be explained by changing consumption patterns: a growing share of grocery spending has flowed out of traditional supermarkets and into alternatives. Cross-border consumption into Shenzhen has surged post-COVID, with Hong Kong residents increasingly shopping at Walmart, Sam’s Club, and Costco for lower prices, while online platforms like HKTVmall and Pandamart have further fragmented demand. In that context, perhaps even after the merger, the ability to meaningfully raise prices without losing customers remains limited. Instead, the merger may be better understood as a response to competitive pressure and a push for efficiency-reducing duplicated store networks and optimizing locations, similar to a Hotelling game where firms reposition to minimize direct competition.

For the average Hong Kong shopper, the impact may be subtle: not necessarily higher prices, but perhaps fewer overlapping stores, meaning it could take a bit longer to find the most convenient store. And if they do increase prices, a supermarket in Shenzhen is just a border crossing away.

Where I think we're headed...

Non-farm payrolls may decrease in the near future (perhaps realising in Q2) due to tech company layoffs and continued CAPEX (as aformentioned). This, alongside worsening inflation data (caused by the energy shock caused by the conflict), may result in worsening economic conditions. In the long-run, apart from investing into AI and data centres, energy or utility stocks may perform well due to increased energy demand.
