Weekly Summary

- Fed keeps rates at 3.5-3.75%, with a bias toward future rate cuts, but 3 members dissented against this dovish signalling (Cox).

- Core PCE inflation increased to 3.2%, reaching its highest level since November 2023. This measure of inflation excludes food and energy prices, and its rise can be attributed to consumer demand and a tightening labour market.

- Bank of Japan keeps interest rates steady despite a sharp rise in inflation. Traders expect a rate hike in June.

- UAE, OPEC's third-largest oil producer, left the cartel on Friday, the 1st of May.

- Meta, Google, Microsoft, and Amazon reported earnings this week. Meta shares fell as the company announced higher CAPEX for AI infrastructure and rising memory costs; Google's cloud revenue beat estimates, and search revenue grew to $60.4 billion USD (Wells et al.). Microsoft Azure cloud revenue grew 40% yoy, and net income rose by 23% (Wells et al.). Amazon's AWS revenue, retail sales, and ad revenue exceeded analysts expectations, but they anticipate higher transportation costs from elevated fuel prices (Wells et al.).

- The defense sector gives up gains accumulated from the US-Iran war.

- Canada establishes a $18.4 billion USD Sovereign Wealth Fund.

The future of OPEC

In Economics 101, perfect competition is great for consumers: firms will undergo fierce competition, and will compete until price equals marginal cost, and so their profits would be zero. But firms really hate that, and so, instead of this intense comeptition, some firms may get clever and form a cartel, pooling together their market power and effectively becoming a monopoly. As a combined monopolist, they jack up prices, restrict supply, and maximise profits for its members. Obviously, cartels are illegal under normal circumstances, but because OPEC acts as a cooperation between sovereign nations (and is primarily used to stabilise prices during periods of shock and volatility), it's considered legal. It's very rare to legally have cartel-like qualities (setting production quotas for members which reduces supply, and setting a price range etc.), and it seems like a great opportunity to be part of, so why would the UAE want to leave?

Some potential reasons why...

The UAE is one of few members in OPEC with spare capacity - having unused oil production infrastructure that can be used to increase supply in the short-term (Al Jazeera). However, OPEC's production quotas restrict its ability to pump more oil out and into markets. They've pushed for higher production quotas before, creating tension with Saudi Arabia, which basically leads decision-making in OPEC. Also, because they're only the third-largest oil producer in OPEC, they don't get much of a say. According to the BBC, experts say the UAE wants more freedom to increase output independently, increasing oil production (Edser et al.).

What does this mean for oil markets and the future of OPEC?

In the short-term, not much changes because the Strait of Hormuz remains closed under the ongoing US-Iranian blockade, keeping oil prices elevated regardless of UAE output - most crude still needs to ship through there anyway. In the long-term, however, OPEC loses much of its spare capacity, reducing its ability to ramp up supply quickly and making coordination between members more difficult to sustain. Moreover, as an independent producer, the UAE will likely boost production, flooding oil markets, increasing supply, and ultimately lowering prices. All this benefits oil-importing economies (primarily in Europe and Asia), allowing them to face softer inflation from energy shocks in the future, albeit at the expense of increased volatility in oil markets.

Brent Crude prices remain largely unaffected in the short-term (Yahoo Finance).

For existing oil firms, lower prices in the long-term may reduce profits, causing a mild sell-off. This also reduces the upwards pressure on CPI, making treasuries more attractive and lowering yields (yields move inversely to bond prices). Other rate sensitive assets may then benefit from cheaper borrowing costs.

Where I think we're headed...

The 3 FED dissenters who have taken a hawkish stance indicate that inflation caused by rising energy costs are likely to continue from the war, causing rates to be on hold for longer than expected or even rise in the short-term. We can see traders supporting this view by scaling back on rate cut bets, causing treasury yields to rise.

USDJPY may move sideways in the near term; it faces downward pressure from Japan's reliance on Middle Eastern oil imports, whilst simultaneously facing upwards pressure from a hawkish central bank. Globally, if such uncertainty and volatility in markets continues to increase, then safe-haven assets such as gold may see meaningful price appreciation as investors stability.
