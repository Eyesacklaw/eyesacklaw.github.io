Weekly Summary

- Trump rebuilds his tariff wall, replacing existing tariffs which were about to expire or previously got removed by the Supreme Court. This, coupled with his threat of planning a "big attack" on Iran, increases trader's expectations of inflation, causing the market to price in a higher probability of a rate hike later this year.

- The Houthis (Iran's allies in Yemen), have attacked a Saudi Arabian tanker on Bab al-Mandab Strait, causing oil prices to surge back to $100 a barrel.

- Learning from a painful lesson as the Strait of Hormuz remains blocked, developing nations are planning to build emergency oil reserves to reduce the impact of future oil shocks on the economy. However, this strategy may be difficult to implement in the short-term due to elevated oil prices, and might not be effective in cushioning its impact on the cost of living.

- In order to compete with rivals, the HKEX is relaxing its listing rules, allowing for dual-share classes to be listed and allow for companies to file anonymously (Sandlund). More types of shares listed in the market. Sounds good right? However, dual-share classes raise ESG concerns as founders may give themselves supervoting rights, leaving the public with little control over the decisions made. Hmm, that sure reminds me of SpaceX...

- More and more ETFs hit the market as small firms try to steal some market share from incumbents (e.g. BlackRock and Vanguard), creating thematic or leveraged ETFs to appeal to retail investors. ETFs are relatively attractive to investors compared to mutual funds as they offer greater transparency, lower fees, and tax advantages (Johnson).

- The Japanese Yen has fallen to ¥163 per US dollar, its lowest in 40 years (McGeever). Traders are now shrugging off officials' warnings of intervention, as like the Boy who Cried Wolf, they have failed to back up their claims of restoring the Yen to a suitable level. This yields 2 opportunities: Firstly, travelling to Japan has basically never been cheaper, and since Japan's budget deficit is getting worse, they will have limited funds to intervene, so the Yen might stay weaker for longer.

Updates on Individual Stocks

- More traders are shorting SpaceX as part of a wider sell off from the AI boom, causing equity to fall below its IPO price and bond yields to increase to junk levels. Investors are also betting that SpaceX will default on debt, causing credit default swaps on the company to increase in popularity. Moreover, insiders can sell shares as soon as SpaceX's quarterly earnings are released, potentially flooding the market with excess supply, and causing its share price to fall significantly. Personally, I'd expect the next few weeks to be volatile, as SpaceX reports earnings on the 4th of August.

Too tight!

The Bank of England (BoE) is going through quantitative tightening by reducing its stock of long duration gilts (bonds issued by the UK government). This doesn't sound bad in theory, I mean, reducing money supply helps control inflation, which is exacerbated by the shock in energy markets from the US-Iran war, but there are unintended consequences to UK debt and the future of government spending.

When a government needs money, it sells bonds to the market. These bonds are then purchased by investors, including financial institutions, fund managers, retail investors (well, at least the older, more sensible demographic), and the central bank. Particularly, if the central bank buys government bonds, it gives money to the government to fund projects, which then flows into the economy, thus increasing money supply, known as quantitative easing. Conversely, quantitative tightening is when the central bank reduces the size of its balance sheet and reduces money supply, either by selling government bonds to the market or letting government bonds mature and then deleting the cash (instead of rolling it over to new bond issues).

What quantitative tightening means is that, since the central bank becomes a net seller of government bonds, if the government needs to borrow for other projects, they will have to sell to other investors (rather than having the central bank absorb it all). By demand and supply, this leads to higher yields.

This might not be problematic for the short-term, but as long duration bond sales already have relatively high yields (from a higher maturity premium) and lower liquidity, further quantitative tightening increases borrowing costs for the government, which in turn worsens the budget deficit. And that may lead to a vicious cycle of worsened sovereign credit ratings, greater spreads, and less confidence in the economy. That's why investors are warning the government to stop.

If the BoE really does follow investors' advice, the most obvious winners would be long-duration gilts. Moreover, if the market believes that the BoE is easing pressure without fully ignoring inflation, inflation-linked gilts may perform well. In terms of equities, falling yields would benefit rate sensitive sectors such as tech (AI), utilities, and real estate, as they require massive CAPEX and interest payments are dependent on rates, not to mention their valuation also benefits from a lower discount rate. In general, anything that is hurt by higher government borrowing costs gets a bit of relief if gilt yields calm down.
