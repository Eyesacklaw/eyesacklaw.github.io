Weekly Summary

- Non-farm payrolls were better than expected, causing traders to bet that the Fed will raise interest rate this year, increasing short-term borrowing costs.

- US inflation increased to 4.2% in May, influenced by the oil shock in the US-Iran war (McCormick and Smith). This was, however in, in line with analysts expectations, causing negligible change in treasury yields.

- I'm not too sure how to summarise the geopolitics between US and Iran. Iran recently shot down a US military helicopter patrolling the Strait of Hormuz, which has prompted the US to retaliate with strikes, causing oil prices to spike higher in fears of the ceasefire breaking. But on Friday, Trump claimed to be close to establishing a deal with Iran, calling off the strikes. Iran has yet to comment on the deal, and so it's hard to tell whether anything actually happened.

Updates on Individual Stocks

- Meta is considering to raise money in a stock offering to fund the data centres needed to train and run advanced AI models (Murphy et al.)

- Most of SpaceX's valuation is built upon assumptions that they can go to mars and build data centres in space. If you ask me, that's a little bit optimistic..., sounds like something straight out of a sci-fi movie.

- Apple's Siri may get an AI upgrade, where they would be able to respond in a similar manner to other chatbots, whilst being able to use your personal information as context. Don't worry, even though your data is passed through Google's servers to be processed, Apple claims that personal data will be destroyed (Acton).

Bearish on Bullion?

The US-Iran war has (obviously) caused oil prices to increase, and as most oil is transacted in dollars, this has strengthened the dollar against foreign currencies. Countries such as Turkey had to sell gold reserves to prop up their currency, which, coupled with higher inflation (of 4.2%) and better than expected non-farm payroll values as aforementioned, increased expectations of rate hikes by the FED. Currently, traders are expected one 25 bp (basis point) rate hike by the end of the year. Such factors have caused the demand for gold, a non-yielding asset, to fall to its 6 month low (Zaidi).

Furthermore, in recent years, gold has experienced waves of heightened volatility from retail speculators, which has increased its correlation with the stock market. During periods of stock market corrections, investors may sell other assets such as gold to meet margin calls and defend their positions from liquidation, causing their prices to decline with the market. Thus, they potentially no longer offer as much of a "safe-haven" status compared to before. Suppose SpaceX's IPO (due later tonight - as of Friday) does poorly and the market declines, in which case gold prices will tumble with it. Moreover, if tech stocks decline in the near future, that would drag major indicies with it, again triggering large scale liquidations.

With that said, gold remains useful within a high inflation, low growth scenario amongst macroeconomic uncertainty and fear (as discussed in the previous article). Low growth hinders central banks from raising interest rates (as it will cause unemployment), whilst high inflation eats away at the real yields of equities and bonds. Amongst the longer-term dedollarisation initiative adopted by BRICS and the increased need for self-reliance, gold remains useful as a medium for exchange. Perhaps gold might not perform well in the near future, and its price may remain stagnant or even decline for the next year, but against the greater econonomic backdrop it does make sense to allocate some capital to it as a store of value, a hedge against uncertainty (nonetheless a weaker hedge than before), and something that can be used as a medium of exchange in a post-dollar world. After all, that's why foreign reserves still remain net buyers of gold.

Shifting to Alternatives

In the past decade or so, alternative investments have generated returns that are significantly higher than the stock market. Bitcoin, for example, has averaged a CAGR (compounded annual growth rate) of 54-94% compared to the S&P500's 9-13% (CaseBitcoin). Luxury watches produced by Rolex, AP, or Patek Philippe have also achieved returns of up to 20% annually, outperforming stocks (The Standard 英文虎報). Select trading cards (including Pokémon of course!) has achieved anywhere between 15-40% CAGR. Of course, these returns fluctuate based on demand for the card, specifications and rarity, but vintage cards featuring popular Pokémon (equivalent to blue-chip stocks), have generally performed well. For reference, a sealed sv4a box I bought one year ago for $280 HKD is worth ~$760 HKD a year later, equivalent to a 171% return on investment. Buying the same amount of silver, I recieved a 124% return on investment over the same time horizon, whilst the S&P500 returned 26.4%.

Trading cards are, in a strict financial sense, worthless. They don't produce cash flows, pay dividends, or compound earnings. And yet, some of them now trade like small-cap equities. Which suggests that what we're really looking at is not value, but demand and speculation.

One obvious driver is that the original customer base has aged into peak earning years. Pokémon cards, once a childhood hobby, are now being purchased by adults with disposable income. At that point, the cards stop being toys and start becoming collectibles, even where price is less about utility and more about scarcity, nostalgia, and signaling.

At the same time, traditional financial markets have become harder to beat. As indexing rises and information becomes more widely available, the average investor has fewer edges. If equities increasingly offer market returns rather than outsized gains, it's not entirely surprising that some capital rotates into alternative assets, where pricing feels less efficient, and therefore, potentially more exploitable.

There is also a simple affordability dynamic. Real estate is expensive, equities feel fully valued, and interest rates have reset higher. Against this macroeconomic landscape, spending a few thousand on a rare card or collectible starts to look comparatively accessible. You may not be able to buy a house, but you can buy something scarce, tangible, and in theory, appreciating.

The more uncomfortable explanation is that parts of this market are starting to look less like collecting and more like gambling (supporting my long-term macroeconomic thesis). The rise of Opika pack opening culture mirrors, in some ways, the growth of 0DTE options and perpetual futures, low time horizons, high variance, and a steady supply of participants willing to get ripped off just for a small chance to win the lottery. The underlying asset matters less than the payoff profile.

The question, as always, is what happens when the marginal buyer changes. If prices continue to rise, younger collectors (such as myself) will be priced out. At that point, the market becomes increasingly circular: adults trading with other adults, at progressively higher prices, justified by past appreciation. That can work for a while. But like any asset without cash flows, the long-term return is ultimately a function of what the next buyer is willing to pay.
