Weekly Summary

- The FED maintained rates on Wednesday, even though the market priced in a 33% chance of a rate hike for that meeting, causing bond yields to rise to 19-year highs. Traders continue to reinforce the belief that 2 rate cuts would occur within this year, with a CMEGroup's FedWatch indicating a 67% chance of a 25 bp rate hike in September (CME Group).

- The FED has also sold Euros to buy Yen in order to strengthen Japan's currency and prevent the Yen from depreciating further. Analysts expect that the Yen is unlikely to weaken in the short-term due to US's commitment, but further strengthening past this point is unlikely, thus having limited upside.

- Hedge funds are required to post more collateral as their leveraged AI bets tumble in the past week. The concentration risk of the S&P500 exceed 40%, at higher levels than in the dotcom bubble.

Updates on Individual Stocks

- ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT), China's top domestic manufacturer of DRAM chips, went public this Monday on the Shanghai stock exchange. Since then, its stock price has seen a 400% price surge, indicating investor's enthusiasm. Even though all manufacturers (including CXMT) face strong demand for chips, analysts are concerned that they cannot catch up with more advanced chip producers such as Samsung and Micron, and lack ability to produce high-bandwidth memory (HBM) (Lee and Kharpal). HBM requires EUV lithography machines, which only ASML produces, and is highly regulated by US and Dutch authorities, so Chinese companies in general do not have access to such technology. Currently, CXMT produces DRAM chips required to produce phones / laptops, and has limited ability to expand into the AI chip market. So is this stock overhyped? Potentially, but until CXMT produces HBM technology able to rival its opponents, its share may be limited in the rapidly developing semiconductor market.

Actual uses of prediction markets?!

Prediction markets are not only used for gambling, they can also be used to hedge very specific risks not traditionally available on the stock market. Suppose you own a solar farm. You might decide to buy a contract that pays out if cloud cover is unusually high. If the sun is shining and there are no clouds visible, your solar panels make money, whilst your bet loses. Yet, if there are dark clouds covering the sky, then your bet pays off, which offsets the lost productivity from your solar panels.

This also works for oil. If you're a shipping company trying to ship oil out of the Strait of Hormuz, you could place a bet that the US-Iran war continues. If they magically reach an agreement sometime in the indefinite future, and everything is peaceful, maybe you'd lose your bet, but you'd be able to ship oil out. Yet, if they keep fighting, then your lost profits from oil transportation can be offset by your prediction market gains. Thus, this lowers your downside and your upside, and perhaps you may be able to generate some stable income. Of course, the usual problem is that if the data is bad or the market can be gamed, your hedge essentially becomes useless.

Roadshow, roadshow, roadshow

A bond's coupon is fixed at issuance, but the yield is not. That's the whole trick: the issuer agrees to pay a set cash coupon, and then the bond's market price moves around afterward so the yield matches what investors actually require today.

So if Meta's data-centre project in El Paso is yielding about 7.5%, that does not necessarily mean Meta printed a bond with a 7.5% coupon and walked away (Chan). It can also mean the bond was issued at a price below par, with a lower fixed coupon, so that the investor's return at purchase works out to roughly 7.5%. In bond terms, "coupon" is what is written on the certificate; "yield" is what the market demands when it prices the thing.

In reality, before a bond goes public, underwriters test demand, then set the coupon and price so the bonds clear the market. If demand is strong, the issuer can sell at a lower yield; if demand is weak, underwriters may have to buy it themselves. Thus, the final result is not just "what coupon did we promise?" but "at what price did those promises get sold?"

So the clean way to say it is: the primary market sets the fixed coupon at issuance, but the yield is a moving target because it depends on the issue price and later secondary-market trading. That's why investors can look at a newly issued bond and say, in effect, "the market made them pay x% for that money," even if the coupon itself is a slightly different number.
