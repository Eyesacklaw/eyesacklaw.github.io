Weekly Summary

- Following a drone strike on an oil tanker transversing the Strait of Hormuz, the US has retaliated by striking Iran's missle and drone storage locations for 2 days. This has weakened the belief that the ceasefire will be sustained by both parties throughout the entire period.

- In retaliation of European countries implementing a digital services tax on US companies, Trump threatens a 100% tariff on all goods.

- Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies have extended their losses as traders think that high inflation and a hawkish FED will lead to rate hikes. Higher rates increase the cost of borrowing, which may be detrimental to economic growth, thus causing the market to turn to less risky assets.

- EU plans to implement a 15% tax on aluminium scrap exports, to protect the waste recycling industry from foreign competition, and to retain critical resources within Europe (Bounds). Again, this falls under the theme of self-sufficiency or distrust.

- Sovereign Wealth Funds and private financial institutions have been increasing investment in AI infrastructure, private credit, and private equity. When savers (who are able to commit a lot of excess capital) provide money directly to borrowers, this bypasses traditional banks and allows savers to negotiate higher returns for taking on greater liquidity risks. Concerns about the health of private credit have been largely ignored by these institutions.

The Next Generation

There is a huge transition happening in wealth management: the clients are changing. As older billionaires die off and leave their fortunes to the next generation, the younger ones aren't just inheriting capital, but a different set of preferences. They are less loyal to traditional family offices, more comfortable with low-cost platforms, and more willing to allocate to things that look less like traditional "portfolios" and more like "ideas" (e.g. private equity, crypto, etc.) (Clarfelt et al.). This trend is likely to continue as the number of high net worth individuals continues to increase globally.

This creates a slightly awkward problem for incumbents. The traditional value proposition of access, discretion, and steady compounding, competes with an environment where information is abundant and execution is cheap. If you can replicate beta for almost nothing, the question becomes why you are paying for anything else. The answer, increasingly, is access to alternatives. Which works, except that "alternatives" tend to behave like crowded trades once everyone has access to them.

From an investor's perspective, there is opportunity within noise. Technically you can spend time tracking what is trending, what people are buying, which narratives are gaining traction, which assets are "in rotation", and try to get there early. Sometimes that works. Sometimes you end up holding the modern equivalent of an NFT. The useful feature of these trades is that they tend to resolve quickly; you usually find out within a year or two whether you were early or just late.

In the long-term, as more capital flows into speculative and high-beta assets, pricing tends to overshoot. And when that happens, the opportunity often shifts toward the assets being ignored. The idea behind "betting against beta" is not especially complicated: if high-risk, high-volatility assets become overvalued because they are popular, then lower-risk, less exciting assets may become relatively undervalued.

In other words, when everyone is trying to get rich quickly, there is usually some value in being willing to get rich slowly. It is not a timing strategy so much as a positioning one. In a market with rising risk appetite and more speculative behavior, owning things that move less (and are priced somewhat reasonably) can look inefficient in the short term, but tends to hold up better against downturns in the market.

Bottlenecks

Recently, inflation has become a hot topic, and AI infrastructure is part of the reason. It is creating huge new demand for power, chips, and grid capacity, while supply in those areas stays tight.

It's important to look at where inflation is showing up: in which sectors, in which geographical locations, in which companies. According to BlackRock's podcast The Bid, a company's pricing power matters more than before (BlackRock). In particular, companies that produce commodities or have sticky demand can pass on higher costs and survive, whereas those which cannot may see lower margins. Thus, to identify investment opportunities, it is important to target these "bottlenecks", firms which produce goods that are central to supply chains, have huge competitive advantages, and face inelastic demand.
