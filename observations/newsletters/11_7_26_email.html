Weekly Summary

- Trump declares the US-Iran ceasefire is over as the US continues to conduct strikes on Iranian military sites (Blake). Trump has criticised Iran for attacking commercial ships travelling the Strait of Hormuz, and displaying a lack of committment towards the MoU.

- However, according to analysts at Citi, Brent crude prices can fall to $60 by the end of this year (Sandlund and Herbert). More ships have been transversing through the Strait of Hormuz either with authroization by the IRGC, or through dark transits (where they switch off their GPS to prevent tracking). Ships have also been travelling closer to the Omani side of the Strait, making it more difficult for the IRGC to attack them.

- Analysts expect companies to grow their profit at its fastest pace since COVID-19, raising concerns for an earnings bubble. People are concerned of the rising costs for AI companies, a decrease in demand for AI, or difficulty in capitalising on AI.

- Investors sell longer maturity AI bonds due to concerns over their unsustainability in earnings. SpaceX's 30-year bond yield has reached a high of 7.3%. Moreover, newly issued bonds have been absorbing capital flows, causing investors to sell off older AI debt in order to reduce exposure to AI.

Yum Cha

Recently, we've been seeing more and more Chinese involvement in the global economy, which is how you end up with a currency that people once treated like a curiosity now appearing in trade settlement, bond issuance, and foreign reserves.

20 years ago, the Yuan had a negligible impact on the global economy - it wasn't really used for anything. However in 2024, the Yuan accounted for approximately 6% of global trade (Deutsche Bank). Institutions (such as Goldman Sachs) and Countries (e.g. Brazil and Pakistan) have been issuing RMB denominated debt, and the terms "Panda" and "Dim Sum" bonds have been floating around. Put simply, Panda bonds are RMB denominated bonds issued inside China to fund green projects, whilst Dim Sum bonds are the equivalent issued outside China, usually on the HKEX.

Why issue them? Because money is money, and sometimes Chinese money is cheaper, or at least available when domestic funding is not. A government or company can tap a new investor base and get access to mainland investors, which usually have a lot of capital to invest due to lower domestic interest rates. They may also pick up a yield advantage, and, conveniently, tell everyone it is participating in the grand project of dedollarisation. For investors, these bonds can look attractive for the usual reasons: diversification, yield, and currency appreciation.

This raises the question: How will this affect the dollar? Realistically, not much in the near future. The dollar still accounts for approximately half of all international payments, and that is not the sort of thing that changes because a few more borrowers decide to use RMB funding. But over time, the system can become less dollar-centric, which may push up borrowing costs for the US as central banks start to hold more foreign currency reserves. US denominated bond yields may rise as a consequence. Moreover, the US may be less able to export inflation to the rest of the world. As the monopolist faces increasing competition, it may no longer be able to set the terms of global liquidity quite as comfortably as before.
