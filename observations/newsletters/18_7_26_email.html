Weekly Summary

- Trump continues strikes on Iran, pushing oil prices higher to approximately $87 a barrel this week. However, he removed a levy on oil tankers transversing the Strait of Hormuz, provided their country makes large investments or trades with the US.

- Inflation falls to 3.5%, below analysts expectations, increasing traders' beliefs of a rate hike later this year (Gupta).

- Semiconductor have fallen in the past week as investors sell winners from the AI boom. This has been exacerbated by momentum trades (where investors buy when stock prices increase and sell when stock prices decrease, believing the rally will extend) performed by hedge funds.

- Small-cap companies are rebranding themselves to have AI names and changing their industry to be more technologically relevant, for a short-term share price increase. However, this price increase is not sustained for long as many companies fall to their previous prices or even lower than before.

- I think I've reiterated this many times: Rich people are bored of traditional investments, and are now looking into alternatives that offer higher returns with lower liquidity. We've seen appreciation in wines, watches, cards, and art, but now they're flocking into the dinosaur (fossil) market. But before you rush to buy your own dinosaur, please understand that there are concerns over market transparency, as there is asymmetric information since buyers are not disclosing whether the skeletons come from a single individual or are a collection, or if any restoration (such as replacing bones with 3D printed or paper mache ones) has taken place (Lescaze). Or in other words: be careful, or you might get scammed!

Patience, My Child

Before screens and APIs, markets were slow and inconvenient. You traded stocks over the phone or over-the-counter, price data was not widely available, and liquidity was thin. If something was undervalued, it could stay that way for a long time simply because nobody noticed, or because fixing the mispricing required actual human effort.

In that world, short-term trading was mostly guesswork. The investors who did well (e.g. Buffett or Peter Lynch) tended to think in long-term horizons. You bought a business, waited for the market to catch up, and accepted that "eventually" might mean decades. There were bubbles and manias, but the durable returns usually went to people with long-term views on the economy rather than people trying to predict next week's headlines.

Fast forward to the present, and the market lives on your phone. You can trade from anywhere, at near-zero commission, with live prices, charts, and news feeds. Liquidity is deep, data is abundant, and most obvious mispricings are competed away quickly. That makes markets significantly more efficient in the short-term, and changes the way the game is played.

Because once everyone has the data, the edge shifts to speed. Trading firms pay to put their servers in the same building as the exchange (Prameswara). They rewrite code to shave miliseconds off latency. They stream every tick, every news headline, every API feed (including of course, Trump's Truth Social posts - which by the way, he's very smart to capitalise off that and sell API access to trading firms) into algorithms built to react first and ask questions later.

In that environment, retailers who think that they'll buy on good news and sell on bad before the market adjusts is mostly fantasy. By the time you've seen the headline, someone with a faster connection and a better machine has already traded it a thousand times. Unless you can run tens of thousands of orders a millisecond faster than their systems, you out of their league.

However, the long-run phenomenon still works. Markets eventually will have to deal with fundamentals, such as cash flows, balance sheets, competitive advantage, and outcomes of economic policies implemented decades ago. Prices can overshoot or undershoot, but over years, they tend to drift back toward what the businesses are actually worth. This leaves retail investors with the one advantage: algorithms do not particularly care about patience. You are unlikely to beat a server sitting next to the matching engine in a race measured in nanoseconds. You have a better chance in a race measured in decades, where we don't ask "who can execute the trade faster" but "who is better at forecasting the direction of the economy in the next 20 years".
