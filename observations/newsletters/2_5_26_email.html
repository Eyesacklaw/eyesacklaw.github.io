The future of OPEC

In Economics 101, perfect competition is great for consumers: firms compete until price equals marginal cost, and profits fall to zero. Firms hate that — so some get clever and form a cartel, pooling market power and effectively becoming a monopoly. As a combined monopolist, they jack up prices, restrict supply, and maximise profits. OPEC is exactly this, but legal, because it operates between sovereign nations. So why would the UAE want to leave?

The UAE is one of few OPEC members with spare capacity — unused infrastructure that can quickly boost supply. But OPEC's production quotas restrict this. They've repeatedly clashed with Saudi Arabia, which dominates decision-making, over higher quotas. Being only the third-largest producer, the UAE has limited influence. Experts say it wants the freedom to increase output independently (BBC).

In the short-term, not much changes — the Strait of Hormuz remains closed under the US-Iranian blockade, keeping prices elevated. Long-term, however, OPEC loses much of its spare capacity, making coordination between members more difficult to sustain. As an independent producer, the UAE will likely boost output, flooding markets and lowering prices — benefiting oil-importing economies in Europe and Asia, but at the cost of increased volatility.

Brent Crude prices remain largely unaffected in the short-term (Yahoo Finance).

For oil firms, lower long-term prices may reduce profits and cause mild sell-offs. This also reduces upward pressure on CPI, making treasuries more attractive and lowering yields. Other rate-sensitive assets may then benefit from cheaper borrowing costs.

Where I think we're headed...

The 3 Fed dissenters taking a hawkish stance indicate that inflation from rising energy costs is likely to continue, keeping rates on hold longer than expected or even causing a rise in the short-term. Traders are scaling back on rate cut bets, causing treasury yields to rise.

USDJPY may move sideways in the near term — facing downward pressure from Japan's reliance on Middle Eastern oil imports, whilst simultaneously facing upward pressure from a hawkish central bank. If uncertainty and volatility continue to increase globally, gold may continue to benefit as a safe haven asset.
