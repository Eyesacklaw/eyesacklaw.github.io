Weekly Summary

- Gold overtakes US treasuries as the largest reserve asset. Central banks such as China, Poland, Turkey, India, and also crypto stablecoin company Tether have been buying record amounts of gold over the past years, driving up its price (Hook and Storbeck).

- The US-Iran war has caused US oil reserves to be at one of its lowest levels; if nothing changed, analysts predict that this would push the price of oil even higher.

- China's manufacturing activity and exports slowed as the economy continues to soften (Bloomberg News). The RatingDog China manufacturing purchasing managers index fell to 51.8. This decline was due to higher export costs and reduced demand caused by the US-Iran war.

- China has been cracking down on oversea shares purchases to prevent capital flight. Regulators such as Futu Holdings, Tiger Brokers and Longbridge were fined due to not complying to the guidelines.

Updates on Individual Stocks

- SpaceX's IPO is hot! Record inflows are recorded into funds and ETFs that provide early exposure to SpaceX. These funds, which have been previously trading below their NAV (net asset value), are now trading at premiums due to the hype (Herbert and Johnson).

- Google increased its equity offering to 84.75 billion USD to fund AI infrastructure and improve their compute capacity. It is the largest stock sale on record, yet various mutual funds, sovereign wealth funds, and Berkshire Hathaway remain willing to buy in.

- Softbank (a Japanese technology investment fund) will invest 75 billion Euros to build AI infrastructure in France.

- Broadcom's stocks fell by 12.6% on Thursday after they gave guidance on revenues that were lower than analyst's expectations.

- Berkshire Hathaway (Previously Buffett's fund), will acquire Taylor Morrison Home Corp at a 6.8 billion USD valuation (Smith and Rajbhandari). Shares at Taylor Morrison rose on the news, but Berkshire Hathaway remained flat. The stock has also underperformed the S&P500 index in recent years - perhaps investors are more excited chasing returns from the tech sector.

AI valuations are getting out of hand

The AI hype has been less about the AI models themselves and more about electricity. Or rather, who supplies it. Over the past few years, the real winners haven't just been "AI companies", but the entire supply chain upstream: chips, lithography, foundries, data centres. Everyone is spending tens of billions on CAPEX to make sure they don't run out of compute, which increasingly feels like the modern equivalent of running out of oxygen.

The nice thing about this setup is that the biggest buyers (Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and Meta) are already extremely profitable. They can afford to treat AI like a very expensive option. Meanwhile, the suppliers get paid upfront, in cash, regardless of whether the eventual use cases justify the spend. It's a gold rush where the shovel sellers are listed equities with 70% margins.

Inevitably, though, the trade has leaked. Anything associated with "AI" in the market gets a multiple expansion, regardless of whether it has cash flow, earnings, or is even a business. Investors are trying to find the next Nvidia, which mostly means buying things that are not Nvidia.

Some smarter investors have reacted in the traditional way: by quietly buying boring companies in other geographies that pay dividends and generate cash today. This is less exciting, but also less dependent on the AI narrative.

Why we need more gamblers in the stock market

Moreover, more money is flowing into passive ETFs which track market indicies. This is great, except that if everyone owns the market, then everyone gets the market return. And if the market increasingly reflects a the same perspective, then future returns may start to look like what you paid for them.

The slightly awkward implication is that as investing gets easier and more efficient, it may also get less attractive. There will be fewer overlooked assets, fewer easy trades, and more capital chasing the same ideas. That's why large companies have been outperforming small companies in the recent years, and now that most people know to invest in the S&P 500, we may expect to see lower growth rates compared to the past decade. That's why institutional and educated investors still need gamblers (I mean retail investors) to speculate on assets, buy 0DTE options, and inevitably cause boom and busts like the gold and silver crash in January 2026. Trading firms have been profiting on such market volatility, generating record profits whilst the unlucky retail trader loses out. Thus, despite how lucrative it seems to make 10x your principal in one day, please don't gamble. Instead, one should consider diversifying their assets, buying into international markets, debt (bonds), commodities, real estate, and alternative assets.

What to do in each state of the world...

Suppose we simply (perhaps naively) categorise the economy based on the amount of inflation and growth that occurs in each period. In each state, the following assets may perform well:

High inflation Low inflation
High growth

- Stocks may perform well due to strong growth in corporate earnings, and thus partially mitigate the impact from inflation (relative to fixed income). Cyclical businesses such as energy, industrials, and consumer discretionary outperform.

- Depending on investor risk appetite (and how detatched they are from fundamentals), inflows into riskier emerging market funds and technology funds may increase.

- Just about everything but gold, since there is no need for non-productive assets (Note that gold does not generate any form of income, and instead acts as a way to preserve wealth and counteract inflation).

- Equities may again outperform due to strong corporate earnings, drawing capital from other asset classes.
Low growth

- Investors would be more focused on preserving purchasing power, preventing it from being eroded from inflation. Safe-haven assets such as gold or foreign currencies linked to gold (such as USDCHF), may see appreciation.

- TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) may also do well. The opportunity cost for buying equity is effectively non-existent due to low growth, and as TIPS are inflation-protected, the real yield remains constant.

- Low growth incentivises central banks to cut interest rates, causing bond prices to rise. With low inflation, the real value of fixed income remains relatively constant. Thus, long-duration US treasuries, gilts, or other sovereign bonds may perform well.

- Additionally, utilities and consumer staples may be able to retain their value. This is because they sell necessities, thus having price-inelastic demand (consumers buy them in every state of the world). An added benefit is that they usually generate stable, consistent cash flows, and are able to pay out consistent dividends to shareholders, which retains their real value due to low inflation.

Currently, I believe we are in the "high growth, high inflation" phase, but one quarter of weakening GDP growth or a reduction in the business confidence index could easily push us into the "low growth, high inflation" phase. As such, it may be preferable to start increasing allocation into gold or TIPS, and perhaps scaling back on those gut-shot, leveraged stock gambles. After all, it's all fun and games until the market crashes, and then it really starts to hurt.
