The debt problem

US Debt has risen to $40 trillion USD, and the cost of borrowing (reflected by US Treasury yields) has continued to increase in the recent months (McCormick and Duguid). To combat this, the US treasury said it will double purchases of long-term debt (with maturities of 10 years or above) to control yields, particularly targetting off-the-run securities. Off-the-run treasuries are securities which are not the most recent issue. For example, if the Treasury issued a 5-year note in 2025, and in 2026, it issues another 5-year note, then the 5-year note issued in 2025 is considered off-the-run (as it is no longer the newest issued), and (as of 2026) has 4 years till maturity.

Anyway, what happened to markets from this announcement? Yields fell, then rose again, as investors are not convinced that the US government can control inflation. The US dollar has also fallen against other currencies, as the threat of inflation and the threat of increasing money supply (through bond purchases) has weakened demand. According to MarketWatch, the recent $16 billion USD auction of 20-year bonds also only drew modest appetite from investors (Wang). The bid-to-cover ratio, calculated by dividing the dollar bid amount by the dollar value of bonds sold in an auction, fell to 2.53.

US Treasuries Yield Curve as of 21/08/2026 ("US Treasury Yield Curve").

Given this environment, I personally believe that the US government would increase short-term debt to fund its repurchases of these long-tenor securities. After all, even though they claim that tariff policies have increased government revenue, most of it is refunded to corporations, and they need to get their money from somewhere. More frequent issues of short-term debt increases its supply, which by basic economic theory, increases bond yields on the short end of the curve (because price decreases). This causes a "bear flattener" scenario, where short-term yields rise and the overall curve flattens.

Bear Flattening of the Yield Curve.

To bet on this, investors may consider a spread trade, where you short short-term bonds (betting that they'll fall in the future from the excess supply) and long long-term bonds (as repurchases aim to reduce long-term borrowing costs), neutralising duration to mitigate the effect of interest rate changes (from the FED) on the overall curve.

As for the overall direction of interest rates, we're currently in a disinflationary period, but this is still above the FED's target of 2% (I don't think they'll be reaching that target anytime soon). If the FED were to increase rates to control inflation, that will only worsen the borrowing situation as bond yields rise, and increase unemployment. Given non-farm payrolls were worse than expected, the FED may choose to keep rates steady in the next meeting until Warsh gets a "clearer direction" of where we're headed.

Tariff updates

Trump accuses 40 countries with "friendlier" tariff rates of helping China trans-ship their goods to the US, effectively bypassing them. Trump also pauses his 50% tariff threats on Canada as he claims that a deal was reached between the two countries, with the signing of documents happening soon.

Walmart

Walmart's shares are down 10%, as sales only rose by 2.6% in the quarter. Management plans to use Trump's tariff refunds to fund price cuts until the end of the year, which (unfortunately) means that shareholders don't get much of that windfall they were expecting. The company has been facing challenges in terms of rising fuel (transport) costs, inflation, and intense competition in the retail sector. Moreover, as structural changes cause consumers to transition to ecommerce, Walmart's physical stores have fewer and fewer customers.

Is Europe going to run out of energy?

Diesel prices have neared its all time high, as supplies are affected by the conflicts in Iran and Europe. Transportation costs appear to stay higher for longer, causing cost-push inflation on goods and services and worsening the global inflation outlook in the short-term.

In the long-term, Europe's oil refinery capacity is expected to shrink by 20% as investors are unwilling to back new projects (Moore). Other regions, such as Asia, the Middle East, and Africa, have rapidly expanded their oil industry, lowering global refinery costs and making it harder for the remaining European refineries to compete (Moore). This raises significant challenges for Europe's push for self-reliance after experiencing two energy shocks in the past 5 years.

Even with consumption patterns shifting and the rapid adoption of EVs, fuel still remains critical to Europe's security and defence (as fighter jets and ships still rely on them), leaving the EU with a few options:

If the EU were to incentivise domestic refinery and shale extraction, they may provide heavy subsidies (and carbon emission benefits) to existing firms and encourage them to expand their operations. The problem with this method is that they've already been doing it, and further encouraging oil production may only worsen the budget deficit, increasing bond yields.

Alternatively, they may choose to diversify the networks and channels through which it sources its oil. This may lead to more long-term, structural contracts being put in place (e.g. Sale and Purchase Agreements (SPAs) or bilateral trade pacts) which would be beneficial for the suppliers. Transportation of oil to Europe would also likely increase, which may translate to higher profits for the shipping industry.

Finally, the EU may adopt renewable sources of fuel for newer fighter models. Eurofighter Typhoon and Dassault Rafale already run on 50-100% Sustainable Aviation Fuel, which is made from biofuels rather than crude oil (Royal Air Force). This structural change may lead to expansion in the renewable energy sector, however due to its novelty, it's much harder to trade on.
