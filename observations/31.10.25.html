Changes in investing methodology...

To me, it has been increasingly difficult to identify opportunities in markets. With more people investing, there has been increased liquidity, and markets, in general, have become more efficient and competitive. Furthermore, I realise that most information about a particular company has been priced in by the market already. For example, an oil company could have a solid balance sheet (with low debt), decent earnings, but is trading at historically low P/Es due to an expected decrease in oil prices (which then, in turn, hurts the company’s revenue/profit for the next few quarters). A company’s fundamentals may not necessarily be reflected in its share price due to macroeconomic (external) influences by the government, because investors take that into account as well.

To make an “informed decision”, one cannot just analyse the business’s fundamentals without combining the macroeconomic landscape of your holding period. But in the short term, I think you may be able to get away with the opposite: analyse how the economy and politics will impact certain sectors, then place “bets” on those specific sectors. I talked about this investment idea with my friend, and he raised a good point: What’s the difference between this and gambling on Polymarket? (Honestly, I don’t see the difference, except that the stock market is legal.)

However, in recent years, I noticed that markets have become increasingly political, from Trump’s tariffs to wars, to Takaichi, and more. I’ve also observed that our world has become increasingly polarised (and thus politics-dependent) with extremism, inequality, far-right, and far-left. Thus, to some extent, there is some reason to “bet” on macroeconomic changes and policies, how they affect certain industries, and the difference between the intended effect of the policy vs. what actually happens. One better way to capitalise on this trend would be to bet on polarisation increasing instead of individual macroeconomic/geopolitical events.

Yet for longer holding periods, I think we can follow the firm-foundation theory (in simple terms, a good profitable company with a durable competitive advantage = good investment), assuming macroeconomic policies do not significantly affect the business’s operations (or put them out of business). But if one plans on doing that, then they must be prepared to hold for extended periods of time. Perhaps that’s why Buffett said, “Our favorite holding period is forever”.

Betting on Polarisation...

With greater geopolitical uncertainty, investors prefer safe-haven assets such as gold, silver, and other precious metals. This is particularly true when investors lose confidence in government currencies (such as the dollar) or would like to protect their wealth against inflation. In our current macroeconomic environment, because of 1) geopolitical instability from the unresolved wars, 2) the FED expected to lower interest rates by 100-150 basis points in the next year, and 3) China's rapidly increasing gold reserves in the past 2-3 years, the price of gold has skyrocketed (CME Group). However, ever since the end of Diwali and trade tensions, gold prices have slowly decreased. If they decrease further (to more sustainable, pre-rally levels), it may present buying opportunities. This is the same for silver, precious metals, and cryptocurrencies (basically anything with a finite supply); however, one must be careful, as the price of silver is much more influenced by industrial demand.

In the event of conflict or war, demand for oil and weapons increases. Capitalising on this may involve purchasing shares of oil companies and defence stocks (Lockheed Martin, Boeing, General Dynamics, etc.), provided valuations are attractive. An alternative would be to invest in an oil ETF such as VDE (Vanguard Energy ETF), consisting of oil production and refinery, thus covering the entire stream.

Where I think we're headed...

De-dollarisation. The use of RMB in global financial trade has increased rapidly in the past few years to 7.6% (Sandlund and Ko). This partially explains the reason for China's increasing gold reserves – perhaps to show other countries that their currency is reliable compared to the dollar. China is also making it easier for foreign investors to buy bonds in RMB, and using low interest rates to entice borrowers to convert to RMB.

Trump has also pardoned the Binance founder5 and has passed several favourable crypto laws during his time in office. In the past year, Bitcoin has also been added to the FED’s crypto reserves. Despite crypto having a bull market, with alt-coins reaching all-time highs earlier this year, I think this bull market will be sustained to at the next presidential election in 2028.
