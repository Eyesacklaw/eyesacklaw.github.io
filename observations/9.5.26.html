Weekly Summary

- Australia raises interest rates to 4.35%, citing inflation and higher commodity prices from the US-Iran war (Fildes).

- Trump announced Project Freedom, where the US will escort ships out of the Strait of Hormuz, then paused the project a day later (Uribe). Oil prices fell due to market expectations for the de-escalation of the conflict; however, such policy inconsistency makes it difficult for traders to position themselves. Perhaps this is a strategy borrowed from Sun Tzu's Art of War, where in order to confuse your enemy you must confuse yourself. But in all seriousness, this has made the week's market summary extremely difficult to write :(

- The SEC proposes to allow US companies to file semi-annual earnings reports instead of quarterly. Although deregulation may reduce regulatory costs for firms and allow them to make longer-term investment decisions (rather than please investors every three months), it raises concerns about the reduced transparency of markets, reducing market efficiency.

Updates on Individual Stocks

- DeepSeek is being valued at $45 billion USD as China's state-backed Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund (known as the "big fund"), plans on investing in it.

- GameStop offers $55.5 billion USD to takeover eBay; Michael Burry, the famed investor who shorted the US housing market in 2008, exited his entire long position on GameStop.

- HSBC profits fall due to indirect exposure to Market Financial Solutions and the Middle East war, causing shares to drop by approximately 5%.

- Shares of Nintendo have been falling due to memory chip prices increasing. Memory chips have been instead used to build data centres, and as Nintendo relies on those chips to build switch consoles, this has increased input costs. There are concerns that Nintendo may have to pass on some of that cost to price-sensitive, young consumers, thus hurting future sales (Keohane).

Thinking Beyond Oil

Everyone knows and talks about how oil prices have risen from the war. It does not take much intellect to state. Higher energy prices leads to cost-push inflation, which raises input costs for businesses and squeezes margins. Over time, those costs are passed on to consumers, leading to higher prices and lower living standards.

Those are the primary effects of the war - stopping here would miss the most interesting parts of the story (and make a really short blog). So what are the secondary and tertiary effects of the war?

Fertilisers. The gulf region doesn't only export oil, they also export urea (yes, it's the compound found in urine), which is used to make fertilisers. As energy prices rise, so does the cost of producing and exporting urea. Consequently, urea futures have risen sharply since the war, which would directly translate to elevated food costs or reduced crop yields. Unlike energy, food inflation would take a longer timeframe to realise, is much more stickier, and affects global supply chains.

Governments, in the face of rising oil prices, have also not stood still. In order to alleviate its burden on consumers, or perhaps improve their approval ratings, they have rolled out fiscal interventions. The EU has subsidised oil costs, whilst Australia removed oil tax to support drivers. Yet, such fiscal policy comes at the cost for more borrowing. If traders question debt sustainability (i.e. think that it may be harder for the government to repay its debt), bond yields increase as they demand a premium. This increases the government's long-term borrowing costs, making it even harder for them to fund future projects.

That's not to mention that oil volatility will damage trade. According to analysts, oil price volatility is more damaging to trade than elevated but sustained oil prices (Foster). Less trade causes less GDP growth, which may lead to a weakening economic environment in the near future. In a stagflating macroeconomic environment (low GDP growth and inflation), safe-haven assets such as gold may outperform.

Yet, it is not all negative. Economic shocks often lead to structural change. Elevated oil prices have strengthened the incentive to substitute away from fossil fuels, and we can see firms and investors increasingly investing in alternative and renewable energy. What might have taken a decade of gradual transition could be compressed into a few years under sustained price pressure.

Also, suppliers are not stupid. The war exposed the risk of energy dependence on the Gulf. From this conflict, countries and firms may diversify supply chains, seeking oil and energy inputs from different regions. So, in the future, perhaps war may not have the same impact on oil prices as it has now.
