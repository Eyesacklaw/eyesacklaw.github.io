2026 Outlook

Economic conditions in 2026 remain fragile, influenced by multiple geopolitical tensions, market volatility, and weakening investor confidence. With interest rates sitting between 3.5% and 3.75% and Fed Fund futures data suggesting little change until mid-2026, policy stability contrasts greatly with the instability seen in global markets (CME Group). Recent labour figures further add to the uncertain narrative; unemployment increased by 0.1 percentage points, whilst non-farm payrolls dropped by 92,000, emphasising softening growth in the economy (Trading Economics “Non Farm Payrolls”; Trading Economics “Unemployment Rate”).

Geopolitical shocks have further intensified uncertainty and volatility across global markets. Trump’s nomination of Warsh as the new Fed chair in late January coincided with a sharp correction in gold and silver, whilst a renewed conflict between the US/Israel and Iran has resulted in price spikes for oil and gas. Notably, worries of supply disruptions, exemplified by the suspension of oil shipments in the Strait of Hormuz and Qatar’s declaration of force majeure, have increased concerns about elevated energy prices, which may lead to cost-push inflation (Crew; Reuters Staff). Despite the recent release of the strategic oil reserves by the International Energy Agency, the market remains unfazed, as analysts believe the releases are insufficient (Hernandez and Lawler). Thus, such inflation may linger and cause higher rates to persist in the long term.

Investor behaviour further amplifies this already volatile backdrop. In recent years, retail investors have shown greater speculation in markets, drawn by “fast profit” opportunities rather than longer-term investments. Particularly, betting platforms such as Polymarket have seen exponential growth in liquidity, expanding from $73 million in 2023 to nearly $9 billion in 2024 (Sacra). As aforementioned, the secondary surges in gold and silver prices, driven by retail enthusiasm and macroeconomic uncertainty, emphasise how retail sentiment can meaningfully amplify asset price movements. Moreover, software and asset management stocks have also been severely beaten down by sell-offs, as investors increasingly perceive them as being displaced by AI. Investors have become more cautious about the technology sector, shifting their focus towards traditional and defensive industries.

In light of this uncertain environment, I believe that defensive sectors such as utilities may be worth looking at; long-term AI data centre power contracts generate relatively consistent cash flows that drive sustained demand, whilst also being necessary for energy security in the event of a prolonged conflict. Gold may also benefit from its traditional “safe haven” status, protecting from inflationary pressures caused by energy disruptions and a lower interest rate environment.
