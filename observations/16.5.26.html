Weekly Summary

- US Non-farm payrolls unexpectedly increased by 115,000 in April, whilst the unemployment rate remained unchanged at 4.3% (Ferreira); this was primarily driven by service-sector hiring, particularly due to increased demands in healthcare, transportation, and retail.

- However, inflation has risen to 3.8%, the highest in 3 years, with core inflation (which excludes food and energy), rising to 2.8% (McCormick and Duguid). Treasury yields rose with inflation as well (this is because investors demand higher returns to compensate for the reduced purchasing power of future fixed interest).

- UK 30-year gilts have risen to 5.79% on Tuesday, increasing borrowing costs for the government, making it harder for them to fund expenditure (Palma and Georgiadis). Their increase in the past few months are mostly attributable to the inflation concerns from the US-Iran war, and is recently exacerbated by political instability surrounding the future of Starmer.

- The Indian Rupee has fallen to a historic low against the dollar due to rising energy costs and the trade deficit, causing (similarly to the case of the UK), an increase in bond yields and long-term government borrowing.

- Lime bike (bicycle sharing company prevalent in the US and UK) files for IPO, however, major concerns regarding its performance include difficulties in complying with authorities, legal disputes with users, and difficulty to generate profit. Competition in the bicycle sharing industry is also sharp, and it is difficult for a company to establish a "brand image" (at the end of the day, they're just bicycles). I think I'll pass on this one.

Sector-specific Updates

- Free cash flow of technology companies have hit a decade low due to increased AI spending, R&D, and data centre spending. Yet, said technology firms have gained the most since the US-Iran war and have been leading the gains in the S&P500.

- Poor performance in REITs due to rising interest rates and falling demand for commercial property.

Betting on Biofuels

The IEA has warned that global oil inventories are declining, pointing to a higher probability of future price spikes (Ratcliffe). Importing countries in Southeast Asia are struggling. Even Calbee (the chips brand we used to eat when we were little), reportedly switched to black-and-white packaging to reduce petroleum-based ink usage (Chia).

What does this mean for us?

Well, markets are forward looking, i.e. all investors want to position themselves before news is announced, so they essentially bet on what may happen in the future. Hedge funds, in particular, are buying into biofuels, such as soybean oil and agricultural processors, whilst also trading more niche derivatives tied to energy and feedstocks.

As we can see, institutional investors and even international agencies are effectively betting that supply disruptions will persist. And they have had huge impacts on the economy already. Headline inflation in the US ticked up to around 3.8% (as noted in the Weekly Summary); if supply remains constrained, this pressure is unlikely to ease.

Perhaps even if tensions de-escalate quickly, the effects will lag. Oil production, transport, and refining capacity cannot be restored overnight, especially with infrastructure damage in parts of the Middle East (The UAE bore the brunt of Iran's attacks on the gulf states). Supply shocks tend to persist longer than expected. Higher energy costs feed into fertiliser, transport, and production, pushing food prices higher and making global trade more expensive, thus creating a relative advantage for domestic producers.

Given this macroeconomic narrative, two theses emerge: Firstly, companies exposed to biofuels and agricultural processing (e.g. Archer-Daniels-Midland Co (NYSE:ADM) and, to some extent, Chevron (NYSE:CVX) through its energy exposure) may benefit from higher energy prices. Secondly, domestic food producers, particularly in the US and Europe, could gain as supply chains become more localised.

That said, higher soybean and corn prices can compress margins for processors, and food producers may face rising input and labour costs. So we'll see how this plays out.

Investing for the long-run

Recently, my father sent me an article by a finance blogger (arguably more famous than me, and with subscribers actually funding his work!) who laid out a compelling macro thesis: a potential market reversal driven by the upcoming SpaceX and OpenAI IPOs. The idea is intuitive. Such listings act as liquidity sponges, pulling capital out of tech stocks and the magnificent 7 (which basically functions as a proxy for the S&P500) and into new opportunities, triggering a short-term pullback.

While I broadly agree with the mechanics of his argument, I disagree with his recommendation. He suggested to pause dollar-cost averaging (DCA) into the stock market, presumably to wait for a reversal and probably "buy the dip". Yet, in practice, this may be difficult to achieve. Yes, markets feel stretched, and yes, IPO-driven liquidity shifts could cause volatility, however it is possible that the market could continue to exhibit resilience. Periods of heightened excitement around innovation could also fuel both incumbents and new entrants simultaneously.

The real issue is that trying to step in and out of the market introduces a different kind of risk: missing the best days. If AI enthusiasm continues to inflate valuations for another year before any meaningful correction, sitting on the sidelines could mean forgoing a substantial portion of returns. And once you deviate from a systematic approach, you're no longer passively investing - you're speculating on timing.

At a more fundamental level, widespread success in market timing is inconsistent with basic finance theory. In aggregate, the average alpha across active investors must net to zero before fees, and after fees, it is negative. For retail investors, the odds are even worse. You are competing against institutions with superior information, infrastructure, and execution capabilities.

Maybe I'm wrong, maybe I'll lose a lot of money when the market does go down in the future, and maybe others (including my parents) will continue to follow such speculative advice, but personally, I think investing involves discipline. By contributing consistently regardless of the state of the world, regardless of whether stocks are at all-time-highs or lows, I think that a good strategy doesn't needs to change. Treat investing like grocery shopping: you buy essentials every week regardless of price, and when there's a sale, you might be able to afford more premium cuts of beef at a cheaper price. However, you don't stop buying vegetables just because prices are high.
